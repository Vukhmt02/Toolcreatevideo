Alfred Nobel's love of chemistry would one day make him the most feared industrialist alive. But in the 1840s, in a cold apartment in St. Petersburg, it was just a boy's quiet obsession.

His family had almost nothing.

His father, Immanuel Nobel, was an inventor and engineer with big ideas and terrible luck. A man who had gone bankrupt in Sweden and dragged his wife and children to Russia to start again, building sea mines for the Tsar's navy.

The Crimean War made the family briefly rich.

Private tutors replaced public schooling, and young Alfred turned out to be brilliant. He was fluent in Swedish, Russian, French, English, and German before he was out of his teens.

What he actually wanted to do was write poetry.

His father was not interested in raising a poet.

At 16, Alfred was sent to Paris to study chemistry.

And it was inside a Parisian laboratory that he met the substance that would define his entire life.

That substance was nitroglycerin.

It had been created back in 1846 by an Italian chemist named Ascanio Sobrero. And it was an absolute monster.

Pound for pound, it was roughly ten times more powerful than gunpowder. A small vial of it could blast straight through solid rock.

But it had one horrifying problem.

It was wildly, dangerously unstable.

A bump, a jolt, a change in temperature, sometimes seemingly nothing at all, and it would detonate without warning.

Sobrero was so disturbed by what he had made that he spent years warning people against using it. He called it too dangerous to be of any practical use, and he was not wrong.

But where Sobrero saw a horror that should be locked away, Alfred Nobel saw something else entirely.

He saw the one thing the world of construction desperately needed.

Because in the mines and railroad tunnels, men were already dying every single day.

The reason Nobel became obsessed was simple.

The only real explosive available for heavy work at the time was black powder. And black powder was weak, slow, and unreliable.

Cutting a tunnel through a mountain with it could take years.

Nitroglycerin could do the same job many times faster, if only someone could figure out how to handle it without being killed in the process.

Nobel returned to his family's business, and by then the family was broke again.

The Crimean War had ended, the Russian military contracts had dried up, and the Nobels had moved back to Sweden, humbled once more.

It was there, working alongside his father, that Alfred made his first real breakthrough.

In 1863, he invented the blasting cap.

Now, this is the part of the story that almost everybody skips, and it is the most important part of all.

The blasting cap was a small device that used a controlled shock, rather than heat, to set off nitroglycerin.

Before this, triggering nitroglycerin was essentially a game of chance.
